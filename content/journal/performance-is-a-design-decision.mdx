Most performance conversations start too late. A site ships, someone runs it through
Lighthouse, the score is disappointing, and suddenly "performance" becomes a sprint —
a list of tickets bolted onto a design that was never built to be fast in the first
place.

By the time it's a ticket, you've already lost most of the easy wins.

## Performance starts in the design file

Every design decision has a performance cost attached to it, whether anyone names it
or not:

- A hero video instead of a still image.
- A custom font with six weights instead of two.
- An "infinite" auto-playing carousel instead of a static grid.
- A page that needs eleven third-party scripts to render a review widget.

None of these are wrong on their own. They're trade-offs. The problem is that they're
usually made without anyone in the room asking what they cost — and by the time
engineering inherits the file, the trade-off has already been made for them.

> The fastest request is the one you never make. The second fastest is the one you
> make once and cache forever.

## The decisions that actually move the needle

In practice, a handful of decisions account for most of the perceived-speed difference
between a site that feels instant and one that feels sluggish:

1. **Ship less JavaScript than you think you need.** Most interactivity on a marketing
   page can be done with CSS and a sprinkle of JS, not a client-side framework running
   on every route.
2. **Treat images as a budget, not an afterthought.** Every image gets a real
   `sizes` attribute and a format decision — not just "drop it in and let the browser
   figure it out."
3. **Self-host fonts and subset them.** A third-party font request is a third-party
   dependency with its own latency, and most projects only use a fraction of the
   glyphs they load.
4. **Defer anything that isn't the first thing a visitor needs to see.** Chat widgets,
   analytics, and non-critical embeds can all wait a beat.

Here's a pattern I use constantly in Next.js projects to keep third-party scripts from
blocking anything that matters:

```tsx
import Script from "next/script";

export function AnalyticsScript() {
  return (
    <Script
      src="https://example-analytics.com/script.js"
      strategy="afterInteractive"
    />
  );
}
```

`afterInteractive` means the script loads after the page is usable — not before. It's
a one-line decision that quietly protects the metric that actually correlates with
whether someone sticks around: time to interactive, not time to "something painted."

## Performance is a form of respect

A slow site isn't just a technical shortcoming — it's a small act of disrespect
toward the person on the other end, who is very possibly on a mid-range phone, on a
mediocre connection, trying to decide in the first two seconds whether you're worth
their time.

Design and performance aren't in tension. A site that's genuinely well designed
_for its constraints_ tends to be fast almost by accident — because good design is
already about removing what doesn't need to be there. Performance work, at its best,
is just design work that hasn't been mistaken for an engineering problem.
